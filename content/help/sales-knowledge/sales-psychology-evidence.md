A lot of sales advice presents convention and folklore as science. ClientTurn draws a clear line between them. Every idea below is labelled with how strong the evidence behind it actually is, and the product only builds on what the evidence can bear.

## Evidence labels used here

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Well-supported** | Backed by peer-reviewed research, including meta-analysis |
| **Conditional** | Well studied, but the effect only appears under specific conditions |
| **Directional** | Real-world data points the same way, but the study design is weak (observational, vendor co-authored, or old) |
| **Weak / not replicated** | Early results that later, more careful studies did not confirm |
| **Not supported** | Reviews find no evidence it works |

## Speed to lead — directional

Responding quickly to a new enquiry is widely associated with a better chance of qualifying it. The best-known source is an observational study of US firms published in Harvard Business Review in 2011. It was co-authored with a vendor and was not peer-reviewed, so the exact multipliers quoted from it should not be relied on.

**What ClientTurn does:** follows up new leads immediately, because the direction of the evidence and common sense agree. We do not quote a multiplier, and you should be wary of anyone who does.

## Reactance — well-supported

When a message threatens someone's sense of freedom — pressure, ultimatums, "you must act now" — people tend to get annoyed and argue back. A meta-analysis (Rains, 2013) supports this well.

**What ClientTurn does:** the assistant is instructed not to argue or pressure, and the objection playbooks forbid manufactured urgency, invented deadlines and scarcity. When someone says no, the assistant accepts it and stops. See [Objection handling](/help/sales-knowledge/objection-handling).

## Choice overload — conditional

The popular claim is that too many options make people choose nothing. A meta-analysis (Scheibehenne et al., 2010) found the average effect close to zero; a later one (Chernev et al., 2015) found effects only under specific conditions.

**What ClientTurn does:** offers up to three meeting times, spread across the available window. That is a sensible default for a short message, not a law of behaviour.

## "But you are free to say no" — weak, not replicated

Adding a line reminding someone they are free to refuse was reported to increase compliance (Carpenter, 2013). A pre-registered re-analysis (Fillon et al., 2023) found no significant effect in the lower-bias studies.

**What ClientTurn does:** nothing special. It is a harmless courtesy, not a conversion lever.

## Reciprocity and trust — use with care

Being genuinely useful and honest builds trust, and that matters in any sale. But reciprocity tactics — giving something away purely to create a feeling of obligation — are a convention, and the evidence behind specific sales tactics is thin.

**What ClientTurn does:** the assistant may only use your approved facts, prices and proof. It never invents a customer name, statistic, guarantee or deadline to build credibility. See [Business Profile](/help/settings/business-profile-settings).

## NLP — not supported

> **Warning:** Neuro-linguistic programming is not supported by the evidence. A review of 63 studies (Witkowski, 2010) found no empirical support, and a systematic review (Sturt et al., 2012) found insufficient evidence. ClientTurn does not teach, implement or market NLP techniques.

The two useful, observable habits that are sometimes credited to NLP — using the buyer's own words and summarising accurately — are simply good listening, and the assistant does both.

## What this means for your own team

- Reply fast, and do not claim a precise uplift from it.
- Remove pressure rather than adding it.
- Offer a few clear options, then test what works for your buyers.
- Only claim what you can prove.
